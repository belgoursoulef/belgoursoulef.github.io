Critical Learning Details

A Critical Learning outcome is developed at the national level by the National Pedagogical Commission (CPN); it is an absolutely essential know-how that we, as students, must master to validate a stage of our B.U.T., but also that companies expect from us to prove that we have the level required by the diploma:

Les niveaux de développement des compétences

Competence 1 : Administer

View Document TP R1.05

Competence Analysis

  • What I did:
    As part of the R101 resource lab work, I studied Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws. I handled a multimeter to practically measure voltages and currents on simple electrical circuits.
  • Why I did it:
    My goal was to learn how to diagnose hardware failures on network equipment and to accurately understand their power supply needs.
  • How I did it (method, tools, resources):
    I relied on my theoretical electricity classes to carry out the practical manipulations. I used a breadboard and a multimeter to test and validate the various circuits in the laboratory.
  • My difficulties:
    Since the lab sessions are done in groups, the lack of time to practice alone was my main difficulty. To make sure I mastered the subject and was ready before the practical exam (CTP), I had to stay extra hours to redo the circuits and measurements by myself.
  • What I learned from it:
    Thanks to this, I understood how to calculate the actual power consumption of a PoE switch or router. It also helped me realize how important electrical protection systems are within network racks.
  • What I would do differently:
    Next time, I will prioritize a modeling and conceptual tracing phase before manipulating. Taking the time to graphically map the circuit architecture is a key step to streamline my measurements and secure my interventions on the equipment.
View Course R1.02: OSI Model and Internet Protocols

Competence Analysis

  • What I did:
    I studied the OSI and TCP/IP models in detail through the analysis of essential protocols: ARP, ICMP, DNS, DHCP and TCP/UDP. I looked into IPv4/IPv6 addressing, coding, and I dissected the mechanisms of data encapsulation directly in Ethernet frames using packet captures and the analysis of addressing plans.
  • Why I did it:
    It was essential for me to understand exactly how information flows in a network. Mastering these Internet foundations is a mandatory basis before being able to configure, design or administer an infrastructure: without it, effective troubleshooting is simply impossible.
  • How I did it (method, tools, resources):
    I relied on the theoretical courses of the R102 resource, RFC documentation and addressing practicals (TD). For practice, I first used Cisco Packet Tracer then real equipment in the laboratory to capture traffic (HTTP, DNS, ICMP) with Wireshark, while crossing these notions with my preparation for the CCNA modules.
  • My difficulties:
    At first, I was completely overwhelmed by the massive amount of information and the impressive number of different protocols to integrate at the same time. Faced with this overload of new theoretical concepts, I often felt like everyone else was moving faster and knew more than me, which made learning quite stressful.
  • What I learned from it:
    I acquired a global, end-to-end understanding of a packet's journey from the application layer to the physical layer. Opening and reading a Wireshark capture now allows me to visualize what is actually happening on the cable and quickly identify the root cause of a network malfunction.
  • What I would do differently:
    In the future, I would avoid rushing to try to remember everything at once. I would prefer to start with small steps, go step by step by breaking down the information, and take the time to properly consolidate each notion before moving on to the next one to better assimilate things in the long run.
View TP R1.03: Basic Cisco Packet Tracer Configuration

Competence Analysis

  • What I did:
    I configured Cisco switches and routers directly via the Command Line Interface (CLI). My interventions included assigning IP addresses, creating VLANs, setting up static routing, and configuring the DHCP protocol.

    Today, I master the basic commands of the Cisco IOS system, and the logic of network segmentation via VLAN has become very clear and natural to me.
  • What I would do differently:
    Next time, I will take the time to document each step of my configuration in a logbook, with screenshots, so that I can replay the manipulations much more easily without having to memorize everything.

View Wireshark Write-up

Competence Analysis

  • What I did:
    I practiced network diagnostics using standard troubleshooting utilities (`ping`, `traceroute`, `nslookup`) as well as Wireshark to accurately locate simulated laboratory failures (faulty cables, IP errors, switching loops).
  • Why I did it:
    Knowing how to identify the source of a problem and report it correctly is essential. This allows for the rapid restoration of service availability and effective collaboration with the rest of the technical team.
  • How I did it (method, tools, resources):
    During the diagnostic practicals (R103), I was faced with various failure scenarios. I had to apply a strict investigation methodology based on the OSI model layers, then write detailed incident reports.
  • My difficulties:
    At first, without a truly rigorous method, I had a hard time quickly distinguishing a Layer 2 failure (switch or MAC problem) from a Layer 3 failure (routing or IP problem). I tested a bit randomly.
  • What I learned from it:
    I ended up integrating a systematic diagnostic method: I first check the physical layer, then the data link, network, and finally the transport layer. This rigor considerably accelerates my resolution time.
  • What I would do differently:
    I would set up a mock ticketing system among students to practice formal and professional incident writing right from the start of the year.

Competence 2 : Connect

View Lab Sheet: RJ45 Cabling and Reflectometry Testing

Competence Analysis

  • What I did:
    I made RJ45 Ethernet cables respecting the T568A and T568B standards. I then verified my work using a cable tester, while studying the physical characteristics and performance of cable categories (Cat5e, Cat6).
  • Why I did it:
    Structured cabling is the physical foundation of any network. If this base is poor, a poorly crimped cable can cause intermittent failures and packet loss that are very difficult to diagnose at the software level later.
  • How I did it (method, tools, resources):
    I put this into practice during the Semester 1 cabling practicals, as well as part of SAE 1.03. I handled crimping tools, pair testers, and relied on structured cabling guides.
  • My difficulties:
    Perfectly aligning the small copper wires in the correct order inside the RJ45 connector, without them moving during crimping, took quite a few attempts before I had a fluid motion.
  • What I learned from it:
    I now know how to cleanly make straight-through and crossover cables. I also concretely understood why the choice of cable category has a direct impact on bandwidth and maximum transmission distance.
  • What I would do differently:
    I would practice on extra cable scraps before assessments to gain execution speed and precision during crimping.
View Report R1.12: Spectrum Analysis and WiFi Propagation

Competence Analysis

  • What I did:
    I studied the different WiFi standards (802.11 a/b/g/n/ac) and configured access points (APs) in infrastructure mode. I also analyzed WiFi signals and channels using scanning software.

    To make this learning even more concrete, I would create a thermal map of radio coverage (heatmap) right in the hallways of the IUT building.
